# From Transferred Cache to Usable Memory: A Small-Model Case Study with Native KV-Handoff Reproduction

## Abstract

Transferring a language model's key–value cache can change another model's predictions without establishing that the receiver can use the transferred information. We report a retrospective case study of frozen GPT-2 and DistilGPT2 connected by task-specific rank-8 residual KV adapters with 147,456 trainable parameters. An order-dependent binary classification checkpoint achieves 31/32 correct predictions on its original development set; an opposite-class cache replacement reduces accuracy to 1/32. A separately trained five-row lookup adapter reports 20/32 exact two-token answers on a held-out test split, compared with 1/32 under document replacement and 1/32 under binding permutation. Conversely, an abstract two-key task has zero top-1 accuracy despite a positive likelihood contrast. An audit of the attempted XOR recombination test finds that all recombined targets belong to one class, preventing its interpretation as a balanced composition test. These experiments do not isolate the cause of the between-task differences. We additionally reproduce the saved ordering checkpoint in an independent native execution path: selected sender caches, adapted caches, and receiver logits agree with a CPU reference within a fixed numerical tolerance on all 32 cases, including the same classification error. A later revision measures single-query and multi-query latency for the ordering checkpoint in a CPU reference environment: transferring the cache is slower than letting the receiver read the raw input itself when the sender's computation is charged against that one transfer, but cheaper once either several receiver queries reuse the same transferred cache or the sender's computation is treated as already paid for independently of the handoff — the latter being the intended delegation scenario, not this report's default accounting. The native executable itself was timed separately and initially found an order of magnitude slower than these reference-environment numbers; the dominant cause was an unaccelerated self-written SHA-256 implementation used to fingerprint the external weight bundle on every invocation, not primarily Rosetta 2 translation as first suspected. Replacing it with a hardware-accelerated implementation, and building a working arm64-native version of the executable (the only x86-64-assembly file it depends on has no architecture-specific instructions), narrows the remaining gap to roughly 2x the reference-environment numbers on arm64. The report contributes a bounded, inspectable reproduction and an artifact-linked account of successes and limitations, not a new cache-transfer method, evidence of general reasoning, or a general efficiency advantage independent of these accounting assumptions.

## 1. Introduction

The engineering question is straightforward: can a sender compute a representation once and provide it directly to a different receiver, without generating an intermediate textual message? The scientific question is harder: what evidence establishes that the receiver uses the *particular information* in that representation?

One motivating scenario for the engineering question is delegation: a capable model has already processed a document, investigated a problem, or accumulated context through tool use, and hands narrower sub-tasks to smaller, cheaper models. Those smaller models still need the relevant context. The usual options are re-sending the original text for the smaller model to process again, or asking the capable model to write a summary, which costs time and can omit details the summary's author did not anticipate as relevant. A transferred cache is a third option only if the receiver can use it, and only if the transfer is not itself more expensive than the alternatives it would replace — both are empirical questions this report addresses for one small model pair, not settled by the existence of a working adapter.

Three claims must remain distinct. First, a cache adapter may be executable. Second, its output may change the receiver's likelihoods or answers. Third, those changes may enable correct use of sender-private information. Neither of the first two claims entails the third. Moreover, successful retrieval of one association does not establish compositional use of multiple associations.

The central thesis is that a distributional effect, correct use of transferred information, and compositional use of that information are distinct evaluation criteria. In this closely related model pair, an observed likelihood effect can coexist with zero correct answers, while successful retrieval in one experiment does not establish compositional use in another. The experiments do not identify a universal ordering of task difficulty or isolate the cause of their differences. The native reproduction makes one positive checkpoint inspectable; it is supporting implementation evidence, not a second claim about model capability or a new test population.

The study is retrospective. Experiments were developed sequentially, with changes to datasets and optimization settings during the research process. We preserve their individual evaluation boundaries rather than describe the entire sequence as one preregistered benchmark. No new training was performed for the originally reported experiments (ordering, lookup, two-key, XOR); a later revision adds inference-only diagnostics against the frozen ordering checkpoint (Section 5.1) and three new, explicitly labeled training runs — a converged rerun of the corrected XOR pairing (Section 5.3), a same-seed lookup retrain (Section 5.2), and a five-seed ordering variance check (Section 5.1) — none of which replace or are averaged into the original stored results.

## 2. Related work and scope of contribution

Cache-to-Cache already learns projections and fusion between language-model KV caches. Accordingly, learned cross-model cache communication is not our novelty claim. [Fu et al., Cache-to-Cache](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03215).

Latent Cache Flow studies smaller cache translators and communication across different contexts. Its scope also prevents treating a compact adapter or sender-private context alone as a new contribution. [Rossi et al., Latent Cache Flow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22863).

Closed-form linear transfer for prefill reuse and heterogeneous dual-cache communication further establish that linear mappings and cross-model interfaces have existing precedents. We do not run matched comparisons against these methods. [Cross-Model KV Cache Transfer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03893), [Dual-Cache Latent Space Communication](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20617).

Most directly, Cheng et al. audit whether cache effects depend on the identity of the relayed example. Their mismatched, zeroed, and moment-matched controls distinguish private-information transfer from generic effects across substantially broader settings. Our content-specificity question therefore also has a direct measurement precedent. Our narrower contribution is the particular small-model task record and native checkpoint reproduction, not the invention of causal cache auditing. [Cheng et al., When Does Latent Communication Pay?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04893).

## 3. Model and interface

The sender is GPT-2 124M with twelve transformer blocks. The receiver is DistilGPT2 with six blocks. Both use hidden width 768 and twelve attention heads of dimension 64. Both base models remain frozen. This is a favorable, closely related pair; results do not establish compatibility across unrelated model families, tokenizers, positional encodings, or attention geometries.

For receiver layer l, we select sender layers [1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11], using zero-based indices. Each selected cache stream is flattened across heads into X of shape T × 768. Keys and values have separate residual low-rank maps at each receiver layer:

```text
X' = X + (X A) B
A: 768 × 8; B: 8 × 768
parameters = 6 layers × 2 streams × (768×8 + 8×768) = 147,456
```

The map can mix head coordinates; it is not merely independent rescaling within each head. Low rank describes the correction, not the total rank of the identity-plus-correction transform. Different tasks train different adapters. The experiment collection is not evidence for a single universal bridge.

The receiver starts with the adapted prefix cache and processes its own query at positions following that prefix. It never receives the sender's prefix as receiver input text. It subsequently uses ordinary causal attention over the imported memory and its own query states.

## 4. Evaluation and controls

We distinguish task accuracy, content-specific likelihood effects, and implementation agreement. For lookup, per-document NLL is the mean negative log probability across the two target tokens. The reported exact score requires both teacher-forced next-token argmax predictions to match. Earlier target tokens are supplied when scoring later ones; the result is not a separately executed free-running generation benchmark. Causal shifting prevents the token being scored from being its own input at that prediction position.

This distinction should not understate the exact-match metric: for a deterministic causal decoder with identical numerics and tie-breaking, if every teacher-forced argmax matches the target, greedy decoding follows the same target prefix by induction. Conversely, a first greedy error makes exact match fail. Thus the all-token criterion corresponds to greedy exact match over the fixed target length under those assumptions, unlike mean token accuracy or NLL. It does not verify a separate cached-generation implementation, stopping behavior, or continuation beyond the target; those were not run for lookup.

The document-level paired contrast is NLL(control) − NLL(correct). A positive contrast favors correctly paired memory. The lookup script resamples these paired document differences 10,000 times and reports empirical 95% endpoints. These intervals concern NLL, not accuracy differences. They condition on one fitted adapter and one small split; they do not measure training-seed uncertainty. Multiple exploratory comparisons were performed without a study-wide multiplicity correction.

The controls answer different questions:

- **Different-document cache:** does pairing matter, beyond the presence of a cache?
- **Zero cache:** does the channel matter, while acknowledging a possible distribution shift?
- **Wrong binding:** does reassignment of values to names change performance when names and values themselves remain present?
- **Changed query:** can the same table cache support another requested association?
- **Position shuffle:** does destroying sequence order before sender encoding impair an order-dependent target?

An exact classical parser/counting procedure given the original input is the appropriate functional baseline for these synthetic tasks. No superior capability over that baseline is claimed. Raw-context transmission and receiver re-prefill are also essential efficiency baselines; Section 5.1 reports a multi-configuration latency comparison against the latter for the ordering task, under two different assumptions about whether the sender's own computation is charged to the transfer, but a matched end-to-end deployment comparison in the native path has not been measured. Degraded-cache controls are mechanism tests, not substitutes for those competitors.

### 5.1 Order-dependent classification and checkpoint replay

The sender sees 32 A/B symbols, always sixteen of each. Class depends on which symbol is the majority in the first sixteen positions; the second half has the reverse majority. Total symbol counts cannot solve the task, while a simple first-half count can. The receiver sees only `Class:` after cache import. The historical filename calls this “mini-GunPoint,” but it is a synthetic sequence task, not the GunPoint dataset.

The saved adapter obtains the following original-development results:

| Condition | Correct / 32 |
| --- | ---: |
| Correct cache | 31 |
| Different-document, opposite-class cache | 1 |
| Input position shuffle before sender encoding | 17 |
| Correct keys, wrong-document values | 4 |
| Wrong-document keys, correct values | 28 |
| Query after 32 fixed filler tokens | 31 |

The different-document condition uses a one-row roll of alternating class labels, deliberately yielding an opposite-class replacement. Its 1/32 result must not be described as an unbiased random-cache baseline. The pattern supports sensitivity to transferred task information and order, within this selected checkpoint and development set.

K/V swaps show a larger degradation from wrong values in this particular experiment. They do not establish that keys are generally unimportant, nor directly measure query–key geometric alignment on their own. A targeted mechanistic check narrows this: for each of the six receiver layers, the actual attention weights over the imported-cache positions at the final query token (`output_attentions=True`, eager attention) are contracted with the value tensor in use to give the cache's contribution to the attention output, and that vector's cosine similarity to the correct-cache condition is measured for each swap. Mean cosine similarity is 0.878 for wrong-K/correct-V (28/32 accuracy) versus 0.864 for correct-K/wrong-V (4/32 accuracy): the condition with the larger accuracy drop does show the more disrupted attention output, in the direction Cheng et al. report for cross-model transfer retention (Section 2), but the gap in means is small relative to the 24/32 accuracy gap. Per layer the two conditions are nearly identical at the first receiver layer (0.532 for both) and both near 1.0 at three later layers; they diverge most at one middle layer (0.816 versus 0.742). This is a two-condition check on one checkpoint, not a multi-point correlation study, and it explains only part of the accuracy asymmetry, concentrated in one layer rather than uniformly across the adapter. The tested reduced-layer configurations achieve 16/32, whereas keeping the first half of heads retains 31/32. These interventions characterize this trained adapter; they do not prove every individual layer is indispensable or that a retrained reduced interface would fail.

The 32-token delay preserves accuracy but is a fixed-filler proxy, not autonomous long-horizon generation. No retention curve or interference threshold follows. A fresh inference-only replay reproduces all 20 fields named `accuracy` in the historical ablation JSON; floating-point summaries differ slightly. The replay is not bit-identical to the historical MPS result.

A training-seed variance check trains five adapters from scratch on the identical train/dev split (only the low-rank adapter's random initialization changes; the unmodified training script uses its own generator for that initialization, so the global training seed does not otherwise affect this deterministic full-batch loop), each for the full 800-epoch budget. Dev accuracy across the five seeds is 31/32, 32/32, 31/32, 32/32, 32/32 (mean 0.9875), matching or exceeding the historical single-seed result in every case. This checkpoint's near-ceiling accuracy is not an artifact of one favorable initialization within this fixed dataset; it is not evidence about variance across different datasets, model pairs, or hyperparameters.

A template-family check probes whether the adapter learned the abstract majority rule or the specific trained token identities. The `LowRankKVAdapter` correction is applied independently to every cache position regardless of which token produced it, so nothing in its architecture requires the exact training vocabulary. The same 32 dev documents, same class labels, and same per-document arrangement were re-rendered with a different single-BPE-token symbol pair (A→X, B→Y) instead of the trained pair, holding everything else fixed. Accuracy collapsed from 31/32 to exactly 16/32 — chance, not merely degraded — with NLL rising from 0.202 to 0.846. The correction this adapter learned does not transfer to a structurally identical task rendered in different tokens; whatever it captured is tied to the trained symbols, not an abstract position-majority rule.

A separate sequence-length check holds the trained A/B vocabulary fixed and instead generates fresh documents at 16 and 48 tokens (half and one-and-a-half times the trained 32), under the same majority-margin task definition and a proportionally rescaled majority-margin range, since a different length is necessarily a different set of documents rather than a substitution on the original 32. Unlike the template-family collapse, accuracy holds well above chance at both lengths: 29/32 at 16 tokens and 30/32 at 48, against 31/32 at the trained length. This changes where the receiver's own query token lands in absolute position space (earlier than trained at 16 tokens, later at 48), a confound this check does not separate from length itself. The adapter that fails completely under a vocabulary substitution degrades only mildly under a length change in either direction; these two failure/success modes are not explained by a single account of what the adapter learned.

Additional local sensitivity checks on that same development set are retained rather than promoted to general robustness claims:

| Intervention on the saved ordering adapter | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Scale both adapted K and V by 0.5 / 1 / 2 | 31/32 / 31/32 / 30/32 correct |
| Add Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.1 times each cache tensor's standard deviation | 31/32 correct for the tested draw |
| Use the tested weak conflicting query | 31/32 correct; prediction flip rate 0 |
| Moment-matched random cache (Gaussian noise per example, per layer, per K/V tensor, matched to that tensor's own mean/std) | 0/32 correct; NLL 11.369 vs. 0.202 correct |

These are a few fixed interventions, not a sweep over failure thresholds, noise seeds, or adversarial prompts. Unchanged accuracy is not proof of equivalence; scaling by two reduces the number of correct classifications from 31 to 30. These aggregate counts do not determine how many individual predictions changed.

The scale- and noise-mismatch NLL deltas above already carry bootstrap intervals; a TOST-style reframing (`papers/kv-handoff/tost_equivalence.py`, a read-only reanalysis of those stored intervals, no new resampling or model inference) reports, for each, the smallest symmetric margin within which the interval sits: 0.161 NLL for scale 0.5x, 0.070 for scale 2.0x, and 0.214 for +10% noise, against a correct-condition baseline NLL of 0.202. These margins are wide relative to that baseline — the noise margin exceeds it outright — so this formalizes what the raw deltas already suggested (no detected harm at this sample size) without establishing a tight equivalence bound; it does not use Cheng et al.'s margin-anchoring device (Section 2), since no external claimed-gain figure exists here to anchor to.

The moment-matched condition follows Cheng et al.'s design (Section 2): it preserves each cache tensor's own per-example scale, unlike the all-zero condition, and remains a real (if content-free) perturbation rather than a substitution of another document's content. Its NLL (11.369) is worse than both the all-zero cache (5.308) and the different-document cache (4.921) reported above, so the receiver is not merely reacting to gross cache magnitude or to the presence of *some* structured content; content-free noise at the correct scale degrades the receiver's prediction more than either degenerate alternative. This is one fixed draw on one checkpoint, not a distribution over random seeds, and it does not by itself explain why moment-matched noise is more damaging than the other two conditions.

A latency comparison was also taken for this task, in the same PyTorch reference environment used throughout this section (CPU, 50 timed repeats after 5 warmup calls), not the native FASM/Accelerate path of Section 6. Three quantities were timed to the same endpoint (the receiver's next-token logits over the class token): the fixed cost of the sender prefilling the 32-symbol sequence plus the adapter projecting the selected-layer keys and values, paid once; the marginal cost of one receiver query against an already-adapted cache; and the marginal cost of the receiver alone re-prefilling the full sequence and query from scratch, with no sender and no adapter. These were measured across four configurations crossing single-threaded versus the machine's default eight-thread execution with a batch of all 32 original dev sequences versus a single sequence:

| Threads | Batch | Fixed (sender+adapter) | Marginal cache-query | Marginal re-prefill |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 32 | 590 ms | 39.5 ms | 327 ms |
| 1 | 1 | 45.5 ms | 15.7 ms | 23.5 ms |
| 8 (default) | 32 | 320 ms | 30.0 ms | 215 ms |
| 8 (default) | 1 | 48.3 ms | 17.9 ms | 25.1 ms |

Two different questions can be asked of these three numbers, corresponding to two different real deployment assumptions, and they give different answers:

- **Fresh-cost accounting** (the sender runs *only* to serve this handoff): total cost is fixed + N × marginal-cache-query, compared against N × marginal-re-prefill, for N receiver queries reusing the same transferred cache. At N=1 this is exactly the single-query case, and cache transfer loses in every configuration tested — by a factor of about 1.6 to 2.6 depending on batching and threading (never faster at N=1, confirmed across three independent single-threaded batch-32 repeats and across all four configurations). The break-even query count ranges from about 1.7 (eight threads, batch 32) to about 6.7 (eight threads, batch 1); batching amortizes fixed per-call overhead, so the batch-32 configurations break even sooner than the more realistic single-request batch-1 configurations.
- **Sunk-cost accounting** (the sender's computation already happened for its own reasons — the delegation scenario motivating this line of work, Section 1): only the marginal costs matter, since the fixed cost is paid regardless of whether a receiver later reuses the cache. Here cache transfer is cheaper immediately, at N=1, by a factor of 7-8x at batch 32 but only about 1.4-1.5x at batch 1 — the advantage shrinks sharply at the more realistic single-request scale because per-call dispatch overhead, not attention compute, dominates both paths there.

Neither accounting is more "correct" than the other; they answer different questions about who pays for the sender's forward pass, and the paper does not adjudicate which assumption holds in any particular deployment. What is robust across every one of the four tested configurations is the direction of each comparison: cache transfer never wins at N=1 under fresh-cost accounting, and never loses at N=1 under sunk-cost accounting. Both accountings, and all four configurations, use the same 32-symbol classification task on the same frozen checkpoint; neither speaks to longer contexts, larger models, or the native execution path.

### 5.2 Five-row lookup: a positive but separately trained result

The lookup sender receives a five-row name-to-ID table with shuffled row order. Names in the actual artifact are Bob, Alice, Ivan, Lena, and Anna. IDs are selected to have two target tokens. The receiver is asked for one name's ID. The script uses 64 training, 32 development, and 32 test documents; it selects the adapter on development loss before evaluating test. The stored run uses rank 8, Adam learning rate 0.01, seed 20260904, and stops after 80 epochs.

| Test condition | Exact / 32 | Mean target-token NLL |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Correct cache | 20 | 1.447434 |
| Different-document cache | 1 | 15.385736 |
| Wrong binding | 1 | 6.776008 |
| Zero sender cache | 0 | 10.462410 |
| Different query name, same table (secondary) | 23 | 1.605134 |

Wrong binding rotates the IDs across names while retaining row order. The correct-versus-control paired NLL differences are 13.938302 for document replacement, 5.328573 for binding permutation, and 9.014977 for zero memory. Their respective 95% intervals are [11.998692, 15.731031], [3.913476, 6.778359], and [8.195748, 9.724291]. These are observed, unadjusted intervals from the stored run.

Together, these results support usable name-specific information in that controlled setting. The secondary query condition tests reuse of a table with another familiar name, not unseen query-template generalization. Critically, this script saved results but not its fitted adapter, so the historical numbers above could not originally be replayed; we source-reviewed its scoring and reported its stored measurements only.

A later revision reran the unmodified script end to end with the same seed, hyperparameters, and split, training a fresh adapter from scratch (early stopping triggered at epoch 70, against 80 historically). The qualitative verdict reproduced exactly: `SQL_LOOKUP_BINDING_SUPPORTED`, with correct beating both shuffled-document and wrong-binding controls. The three degradation controls reproduced closely (shuffled-document, wrong-binding, and zero-sender exact accuracy all matched their historical values to within one example). The primary correct-condition number did not: 16/32 exact accuracy against the historical 20/32, with query-name-swap secondary accuracy 21/32 against 23/32. Unlike the ordering checkpoint, which loads frozen, byte-identical weights, this is a fresh training run from the same seed, and early-stopping timing is sensitive to small numerical differences across library versions and hardware that accumulate over 70-80 gradient steps; the same seed does not guarantee the same trained adapter. The direction of the finding (usable, binding-specific transfer, not merely document presence) replays; the exact accuracy figure does not, and should not be read as more precise than a single-run, non-bit-identical retraining supports.

A further later revision checked whether the finding survives a less synthetic document surface form, and in doing so found that the historical and replayed numbers above were themselves not as representative as a single additional replay had suggested. The original and replay documents are literal "name -> id" arrow tables; the sender never sees anything resembling natural language. A new training run rewrote each row as a plain sentence, "Bob has ID 12345", newline-joined, instead of "Bob -> 12345" (`lookup_natural_language`), keeping every other part of the recipe fixed. At its one initial seed this reproduced the qualitative verdict, `SQL_LOOKUP_BINDING_SUPPORTED`, at 8/32 exact accuracy — lower than the table format's 20/32 and 16/32, but still the strong verdict.

That single seed did not generalize, and neither, it turned out, did the arrow-table format's own apparent robustness. Two five-seed adapter-initialization variance checks (`lookup_seed_variance` for the original arrow-table wording, `lookup_natural_language_seed_variance` for the sentence wording; both otherwise identical to each other and to Section 5.1's five-seed ordering check, and both still at the original recipe's learning rate) give the real comparison the single historical and replayed numbers could not: the arrow-table format reaches `SQL_LOOKUP_BINDING_SUPPORTED` on 4 of 5 seeds (accuracy 8, 9, 14, 15, 16 out of 32; mean 12.4/32, 38.75%; the one failure lands on `DOCUMENT_SPECIFIC_NO_BINDING` at 9/32), while the natural-language wording reaches it on only 1 of 5 (accuracy 0, 0, 0, 5, 8 out of 32; mean 2.6/32, 8.1%; the four failures also land on `DOCUMENT_SPECIFIC_NO_BINDING` — correct beats shuffled-document but not wrong-binding, meaning the adapter distinguishes this document from another one without correctly routing which id belongs to which name). Both distributions include failures; neither is the clean, single-seed picture either format's individual replay had suggested: a single comparison looking favorable, or unfavorable, is not evidence of a stable effect until checked against a distribution.

That comparison still left open why the natural-language wording failed more often, and two more mundane explanations needed ruling out before treating it as a fact about wording. The natural-language document is about 20% longer in tokens than the arrow-table format (31 against 26, for the same five-name table): perhaps the rank-8 adapter lacks the capacity to route the extra tokens. A rank sweep (`lookup_natural_language_rank_sweep`: ranks 8, 16, 32, 64, two seeds that fail at rank 8) rules this out — exact accuracy stays at 0/32 at every rank, and NLL gets slightly worse, not better, with more parameters, the opposite of what a capacity bottleneck predicts. A learning-rate sweep on the same two seeds (`lookup_natural_language_lr_sweep`: 1e-2, 3e-3, 1e-3, 3e-4, rank held at 8) finds the actual cause: the recipe's learning rate, 1e-2, was carried over unchanged from the arrow-table format and drives training on the longer natural-language documents into a collapsed, near-zero-accuracy regime (NLL around 6, close to the zero-sender floor); lowering it to 1e-3 recovers substantial accuracy on both previously-failing seeds (7/32 and 6/32, from 0/32 and 0/32).

Rerunning the five-seed check for both wordings at this tuned learning rate changes the picture again, in both directions. The arrow-table format, previously only ever measured at 1e-2, jumps to 5 of 5 seeds `SUPPORTED` at mean 23.8/32 (74%, range 20-27/32) — meaningfully higher than every number reported for this task so far, including the original historical run; 1e-2 was never a well-tuned learning rate for this task, arrow-table included. The natural-language wording (`lookup_natural_language_tuned_lr_seed_variance`) also changes, but differently: instead of the noisy 1-of-5 pattern at 1e-2, all five seeds now converge consistently to `DOCUMENT_SPECIFIC_NO_BINDING` at 6-7/32 (18-22%). The earlier instability was masking a stable underlying result, not a fragile positive one: properly tuned, this wording reliably teaches the receiver to recognize which document a query concerns, and reliably fails to teach it which specific id is bound to which name.

That stable failure motivated asking what specifically about the natural-language wording causes it, rather than treating "natural language" as the explanatory variable. A document-form matrix (`lookup_document_form_matrix`, five seeds per form, all at the tuned learning rate) tested a token-count gradient of row separators between the two extremes: "Bob: 12345" and "Bob is 12345" tokenize to exactly the same per-line length as "Bob -> 12345" (verified against the tokenizer before training, not assumed), while "Bob has ID 12345" adds 5 tokens and "Bob's ID is 12345" adds 10. (A sixth candidate, "Bob=12345", was dropped: GPT-2's BPE merges "=" with certain digit patterns inconsistently, breaking the constant-token-length invariant the training pipeline requires across the full id pool — caught by that pipeline's own assertion, not a subtle numerical difference.) The three token-length-matched forms all reach 5 of 5 seeds `SUPPORTED`, at comparable accuracy: arrow mean 23.8/32 (74%), colon 21.8/32 (68%), and — the result this matrix was built to get — "is", a genuine natural-language sentence, 22.4/32 (70%). The two longer forms do not: "has ID" (+5 tokens) reaches 0 of 5, tightly at 6-7/32 (19%, matching the tuned-LR five-seed check above, which used this same wording); "'s ID is" (+10 tokens) reaches 2 of 5, with a wide, bimodal spread (1, 2, 4, 14, 15 out of 32) rather than a stable rate. Document surface form does affect learnability here, but the operative variable is not "natural language" versus "synthetic table" — it is token distance between key and value in the cache. A genuinely natural sentence at the arrow-table's own token length is exactly as learnable as the arrow table; the earlier finding that the natural-language wording specifically underperforms was real but mislabeled, confounded first by an untuned learning rate and then by a wording choice ("has ID") that was natural but also longer, not by naturalness itself. This is one adapter architecture, one rank, one small table size; whether the same token-distance sensitivity holds at other scales is not tested here.

Lookup's table-and-multiple-queries structure also gives a second, independent latency check complementing Section 5.1's ordering-task one, this time cycling through genuinely distinct downstream questions (each of the five names) against one cached table rather than repeating the identical query. In the same PyTorch reference environment, single-threaded, batch size one: sender-plus-adapter fixed cost is 55 ms, one receiver query against the cached table is 17 ms, and one full re-prefill of the table and that query from scratch is 35 ms, giving a sunk-cost speedup of 2.1x at the first reused query — larger than ordering's 1.4-1.5x at the analogous batch-one configuration, plausibly because the table is proportionally larger relative to its query than ordering's single "Class:" token is relative to its 32-symbol sequence, so skipping table re-processing saves more per query. At the machine's default eight-thread execution the sunk-cost speedup is smaller, 1.6x, echoing the same batching/threading sensitivity seen in Section 5.1. This is one cached table and one trained adapter, not a distribution over tables or seeds.

### 5.3 Where the evidence stops

An abstract two-key experiment reports zero top-1 accuracy even with correct memory. Its correct-cache NLL is 6.076248, versus 6.263900 with a different document. The small pairing contrast has interval [0.122524, 0.256115], but the wrong-key contrast spans zero, [−0.315573, 0.311536]. Thus a statistically detectable likelihood effect does not establish usable retrieval. The failure also does not isolate a specific addressing defect because correct-condition recall itself fails.

A separately trained XOR experiment reports 22/32 on its original development inputs and 14/32 on recombined halves. The stored half-swap accuracy interval is [0.28125, 0.625]. However, a subsequent data-only audit finds a defect: `select_docs` cycles through (0,0), (0,1), (1,1), (1,0), and `build_half_swap_batch` pairs the first bit of each document with the second bit of its successor. Every resulting XOR label is 1. The exact 32-document reconstruction gives sixteen (0,1) and sixteen (1,0) combinations, with no class-0 targets. A constant-one predictor therefore scores 32/32. Uniform random guessing still has expected accuracy 50%, but it is not an adequate baseline for this degenerate evaluation population.

The historical score and interval are retained, not recomputed or erased. The stored run records 800 epochs, development-accuracy selection and best checkpoint epoch 180; current training-budget defaults in the script have since changed and are not a pinned reproduction recipe for that historical training run. The pairing defect follows directly from the selection and recombination functions, independently of those budget defaults. We retract the interpretation of 14/32 as a clean negative result on a balanced compositional-generalization test: that intended test was invalid as originally run, and the broader generalization claim remains unresolved.

A balanced recombination pairing is available: pairing each document's first half with a different document's second half at a two-document offset, rather than a one-document offset, yields sixteen class-0 and sixteen class-1 recombined targets under the same selection procedure, verified independently of model inference by `python3 papers/kv-handoff/check_xor_design.py` (best-constant-predictor accuracy 16/32, i.e. chance, rather than 32/32). The 22/32 original-dev and 14/32 half-swap numbers above were computed under the defective pairing and have not been recomputed under the balanced one at matching scale.

A training run under the balanced pairing, at a different data scale than the historical run (256 training documents, 800 epochs, one seed), converged well before the epoch budget was exhausted (best checkpoint at epoch 180; the dev-accuracy history was no longer improving at cutoff). It reached correct-cache accuracy 30/32 and a balanced half-swap accuracy of 29/32 with bootstrap interval [0.8125, 1.0] — both numbers well above chance and, on their own, consistent with compositional generalization. The full criterion set is not met, however: `half2_only` (half1's cache zeroed) alone reaches 27/32, and the bootstrap interval for whether correct beats half2_only on NLL includes zero, [−0.035, 0.657]. The adapter cannot be said to need both halves combined nonlinearly when one half alone recovers most of the correct-condition accuracy; the high half-swap score is then also consistent with the same single-half shortcut rather than genuine recombination. The run's own verdict is accordingly `KV_COMPOSITIONALITY_XOR_NOT_SUPPORTED` — a converged, specific negative result, not the earlier training-budget ambiguity, and not a repaired replacement for the historical 14/32 score, which remains a separate, invalid-as-computed measurement under the defective pairing. This run is not a replay of the original XOR checkpoint and uses a different training-data scale.

The half-swap intervention splices halves of the original symbol sequences and re-encodes the complete new sequence with the sender. It does not concatenate independently computed KV caches; cache composability is untested. For XOR, the separate position-shuffle NLL contrast has interval [−0.464881, 3.600663], crossing zero. The clearer ordering-task shuffle result therefore cannot be attributed to XOR as well. Neither shuffle establishes exact preservation of positional representations.

Lookup success and abstract two-key failure are not contradictory measurements of an identical condition. Their tasks and training procedures differ. The available record does not isolate which difference causes the outcome. Similarly, changes to optimization regimes elsewhere in the project cannot retroactively invalidate or repair these experiments without new controlled comparisons.

## 6. Native reproduction of the saved ordering checkpoint

The runnable artifact uses the project's C primitives, Accelerate matrix multiplication, self-written tokenizer and safetensors reader, and canonical FASM cached-attention executor. An additive layer-count loader supports the six-layer receiver while preserving the original twelve-layer entry point. The executable does not invoke PyTorch. An external CPU/eager PyTorch environment prepares reference tensors and losslessly exports the receiver's original weight container to safetensors; both models and the bridge are SHA-pinned.

For each of the same 32 development sequences we compare selected sender K/V, adapted K/V before receiver query processing, and all 50,257 output logits. This covers 768 cache tensors and 32 logit tensors. The fixed acceptance rule is:

```text
abs(native − reference) <= 0.001 + 0.0001 * abs(reference)
```

Non-finite values fail. All cases pass; the largest absolute difference is 0.00103759766, allowed by the combined bound. All 32 predicted tokens match, including the reference's one classification error. This distinguishes successful software reproduction from an improvement in model accuracy.

The import copies into receiver-owned storage, leaves the sender intact, requires an empty receiver, and commits cache positions only after validating the transformed cache. Tests cover occupied import, inconsistent/full/empty positions, non-finite inputs, overflow rollback, ownership and malformed artifact metadata. Two identical native invocations produce identical output. Existing GPT-2 CLI and cached/full differential gates also pass. The handoff gate additionally passed from an exported copy of the exact staged Git tree before commit 1453cbd2, with the external bundle supplied explicitly.

This is a bounded standalone executable, not a general cache ABI or a new `tensorctl` subcommand. Its capacity is 64 positions. The demonstrated 32-symbol prompt occupies 35 prefix positions; `Class:` extends the receiver to 37. At this receiver geometry, FP32 KV storage is 36,864 bytes per position, or 1,290,240 bytes for that prefix, excluding other buffers. That arithmetic is not a memory benchmark. Avoiding intermediate text generation does not imply lower communication volume than sending token IDs.

A later revision measured this executable's wall-clock latency directly, ten repeated cold invocations after two warmup runs on the same host used for Section 5.1's PyTorch-reference-environment numbers. The initial result was not favorable to the native path: a median 7241 ms through the checked Python launcher and 6697 ms invoking the binary directly (skipping the launcher's own redundant re-hash of the bundle, though the binary still fingerprints sender, receiver, and bridge internally on every call, by design), against roughly 600-700 ms for the equivalent PyTorch cache-transfer path in Section 5.1 (`native_latency`, historical).

That measurement's own causal attribution was wrong. Isolating this project's own SHA-256 implementation specifically, not the system `shasum` used for the earlier estimate, over the same sender+receiver+bridge files (`scratchpad/kv_transfer_audit_20260913/sha256_isolation_bench.c`) found it responsible for the large majority of the binary's wall time: about 5457 ms of a roughly 6-7 s x86_64-under-Rosetta run, and about 4438 ms even natively on arm64. `fasm/spikes/tensor_sha256.h` was a minimal self-written scalar implementation with no hardware acceleration; it has been replaced with Apple's CommonCrypto (hardware-accelerated on both architectures), same public function signatures and semantics, byte-identical output verified against the existing differential gate's independent Python-`hashlib` oracle vectors. Isolated hashing time on the same files dropped to about 3000 ms under Rosetta and about 551 ms on arm64.

The earlier claim that no arm64 build was possible was also wrong, for a narrower reason than a straightforward FASM-to-ARM rewrite. `fasm/spikes/tensor_transformer_executor_f32.asm` is genuine x86-64 assembly, but the only function this executable calls from it is a 34-line generic function-pointer dispatch loop with no SIMD or other architecture-specific instructions. Substituting the portable-C equivalent already used elsewhere in this project for the same purpose (`tensor_killer_mnist_native.c`'s pattern; here, `fasm/spikes/tensor_transformer_executor_f32_native.c`) yields a working arm64-native build (`scripts/build-kv-handoff-arm64.sh`, parallel to and not replacing the original x86_64/FASM build script) that passes the same 32-case verification with the same worst-case error bound.

With the hashing fix and the arm64 build both in place, ten-repeat median wall-clock latency is 5437 ms (launcher) / 4697 ms (direct) for the x86_64-under-Rosetta binary, and 1913 ms (launcher) / 1361 ms (direct) for the arm64-native binary (`native_latency_fixed_sha256`, `native_latency_arm64`) — roughly 1.3-1.4x and 4.9-5.0x faster than the original x86_64-under-Rosetta measurement, respectively, and the arm64 number is now about 2x the PyTorch reference environment rather than an order of magnitude slower. A reasonable further suspicion is that the file is still read twice (once to fingerprint it, once to parse tensor values into memory) and that removing this redundancy would close more of the remaining gap; three independent implementations that eliminate it (memory-mapped access, a single bulk read into one buffer, and a fused single-pass hash-and-parse) were each built, verified against the same differential gates, and measured, and none showed improvement over the fix above, so none is included here. The remaining gap to the reference environment is not attributed to a further identified, fixable inefficiency by this report. These numbers characterize two specific binaries on one specific host; they do not establish what either architecture would measure elsewhere, and no native path exists yet for the receiver's own re-prefill to compare against directly.

That whole-process number is dominated by weight loading, which the rest of this section already isolates. Decomposing the transfer itself, the same way Section 5.1 decomposes the PyTorch-reference path, into a fixed cost (sender prefill of the 32-symbol sequence plus `handoff_import`, paid once) and a marginal cost (one receiver query against the already-imported cache), timed in-process across ten repeats after weight loading and two warmup repeats (`native_amortized_latency_arm64`, `native_amortized_latency_x86_64`), gives a different picture: fixed/marginal median 364/14 ms on arm64 and 441/17 ms under x86_64-under-Rosetta, against 590/39 ms for the single-threaded PyTorch-reference-environment equivalent (Section 5.1's `amortized_latency`, batch size 32). The native transfer computation itself, once weight loading is excluded, is faster than the PyTorch reference on both architectures, not slower — the earlier "native is slower" finding was entirely a property of cold-process weight loading, not of the transfer computation the rest of this report's efficiency claims are about. No native re-prefill counterpart exists to decompose the same way, so this does not extend to a native breakeven-query-count claim analogous to Section 5.1's.

## 7. Limitations and interpretation

The principal limitations are the single closely related model pair, tiny synthetic datasets, task-specific adapters, development selection for ordering and XOR, and missing saved lookup weights (a same-seed retrain reproduces the lookup verdict but not its exact accuracy). Five-seed variance checks for lookup (Section 5.2) first found the training recipe's learning rate, inherited unchanged from the arrow-table format, had never been tuned and was suppressing accuracy for every document wording tested, including arrow-table itself; retuned, a document-form matrix found learnability tracks token distance between key and value rather than natural-language wording as such, with a natural sentence at the arrow-table's own token length exactly as learnable as the table. This is one adapter architecture, one rank, and one small table size — whether the same token-distance sensitivity or the same learning-rate mistuning generalizes to other tasks in this report is untested, and the ordering task's own hyperparameters were not revisited under the same scrutiny. Original result JSON files provide summaries rather than a complete independently regenerated per-example statistical audit. Tokenization and prompt familiarity may affect outcomes. A moment-matched random-cache control, a multi-configuration re-prefill latency and cache-reuse comparison, an alternate-symbol template-family check, and a sequence-length check at 16 and 48 tokens are reported for the ordering task only (Section 5.1); five-seed adapter-initialization variance checks are reported for ordering (Section 5.1) and, across several document wordings and two learning rates, for lookup (Section 5.2); a second, independent latency and cache-reuse comparison is reported for lookup (Section 5.2). The native executable's own latency was also measured directly (Section 6); an initial order-of-magnitude gap to the PyTorch reference environment was traced to an unaccelerated self-written SHA-256 implementation rather than to Rosetta 2 translation as first suspected, and fixing it plus building a working arm64-native binary narrowed the gap to roughly 2x on arm64, with a remaining gap this report does not attribute to a further identified, fixable cause. Independent (non-substitution) template families, seed variance for the other tasks, and a matched native cache-reuse comparison analogous to Section 5.1's are absent.

The positive results establish task-conditioned use of transferred information, not an information-theoretic channel capacity. A 32-symbol classification input can yield just one task-relevant bit. Reduced-head tolerance is not a compression benchmark; fixed-filler retention is not durable agent memory. Native verification establishes agreement with the chosen oracle on tested inputs, not universal numerical equivalence or semantic correctness of arbitrary imported memory. Cache shape validity is not evidence that its content is trustworthy.

We therefore separate three conclusions. The ordering bridge is implemented and reproducible. Stored lookup evidence supports a more selective use of memory, but lacks same-checkpoint replay. A broad advantage over text/context transfer, classical algorithms, other cache translators, or more capable models remains unestablished. None of these conclusions requires treating the project as a new reasoning architecture.

## 8. Conclusion

In this GPT-2-to-DistilGPT2 case study, transferred caches can support order-dependent classification and, with a different trained adapter, selective lookup. Those successes coexist with unusable two-key recall; the attempted XOR recombination evaluation has a one-class design defect and does not settle compositional generalization. The causal explanation of the between-task variation is unresolved. A native reconstruction reproduces one saved ordering adapter through intermediate caches and final logits without retraining. The resulting artifact provides a concrete basis for checking one learned channel; it does not collapse mechanism, generalization, and efficiency into a single success claim.

## Appendix A. Evidence map and boundaries

This map replaces a single sequence of checkmarks such as “capacity -> addressing -> composition.” Those labels mix different constructs and experiments. **Observed** denotes a measurement in the specified setting, **not established** denotes an inference the evidence does not support, and **not tested here** denotes a missing controlled measurement in the report's evidence set. It is not a new experimental verdict or a complete audit of every historical spike.

| Question | Evidence and boundary |
| --- | --- |
| Content-specific use | Observed in ordering and stored lookup; abstract two-key shows a smaller likelihood contrast without usable answers. Not a universal result across all adapters. |
| Addressing / binding / selective retrieval | Lookup supports name-specific retrieval under binding and query interventions. Failed two-key recall does not isolate an addressing defect. These are different conditions, not a logical contradiction. |
| Global versus local information | Both a global classification and one local lookup experiment succeed. The cause of between-experiment differences is not isolated. |
| Value compatibility / query–key alignment | Wrong-V replacement hurts ordering more than wrong-K replacement. An attention-output cosine check (Section 5.1) finds the wrong-V condition's attention output is somewhat more disrupted than wrong-K's, concentrated at one of six layers, consistent in direction but small in magnitude relative to the accuracy gap; not a full attention-score alignment analysis or a measured fraction of information in V. |
| Position / order | Input permutation affects ordering; the XOR position-shuffle NLL interval includes zero. Exact position preservation is not established. |
| Layer participation / partial cache | Tested first-layer, last-layer and first-half-layer configurations underperform the full ordering interface. Not exhaustive leave-one-out tests; no retrained reduced interface. “All six individually necessary” is unsupported. |
| Head participation / redundancy | The tested first-half-head mask retains accuracy. No general head correspondence, rank, or compression conclusion follows. |
| Scale / noise / conflict | Local checks are reported in Section 5.1. A TOST-style reframing of the existing scale/noise bootstrap intervals reports the smallest margin each sits within (0.070-0.214 NLL), wide relative to the 0.202 baseline; not a tight equivalence bound or a strong contradiction-handling result. |
| Persistence | Fixed filler delay preserves development accuracy. Autonomous generation, long-horizon retention curves and interference limits are not established. |
| Composition / cache merging | The XOR recombination procedure as originally computed created only class-1 targets, an artifact of its pairing logic rather than of the task; its balanced-test interpretation was invalid, not a clean negative capability result. A converged rerun under a balanced pairing (Section 5.3) is a genuine negative result, but for a specific, diagnosed reason (a single-half shortcut), not proof that no adapter could combine both halves. Ready-made cache concatenation, multiple senders and incremental updates are not tested here. |
| Bandwidth / capacity | Input length and successful labels are not information-theoretic capacity measurements. Neither 32 symbols nor a five-row table implies complete recovery of all input information. |
| Task dependence / evaluation sensitivity | Heterogeneous results are observed, but tasks, optimization and scoring differ. No matched factorial experiment identifies naturalness, semantic complexity or instrument sensitivity as the cause. |
| Generalization | Lookup has a held-out document split and a secondary familiar-name query. A single-symbol-substitution template check on ordering (Section 5.1) collapses to chance, indicating the adapter did not learn a token-independent rule; a sequence-length check on the same adapter (16/48 tokens) instead holds well above chance, so the failure is vocabulary-specific, not a general fragility to any input change. Independent ordering tests with genuinely new template families remain absent; training-seed replication now exists for ordering (five seeds, Section 5.1) but not the other tasks. |
| Optimization / sample or adapter complexity | No matched optimization, data-size or adapter-capacity study is included here. Earlier hidden-state-injection rungs must not be pooled with these KV experiments as one capacity curve. No universal optimization fix is claimed. |
| Geometry / precision | One fixed 12-to-6 layer selection is exercised, with equal head geometry and related tokenizers. Alternative mappings, unequal head widths/counts, RoPE transfer, FP16 dependence and unrelated model families are not tested here. |
| Causal necessity / wrong memory | Chosen cache replacements affect outputs and pairing scores. This does not prove every component necessary or establish calibrated rejection of wrong memory. |
| Efficiency / deployment | Native agreement is tested. A four-configuration, PyTorch-reference-environment comparison for the ordering task (Section 5.1) found cache transfer 1.4x-2.6x slower than receiver re-prefill at one query if the sender's cost is charged to that query, but 1.4x-8.3x faster at one query if the sender's cost is already sunk, with break-even at roughly 2-7 reused queries under the former accounting; a second such comparison on lookup (Section 5.2), cycling through genuinely distinct queries against one cached table, finds a larger sunk-cost speedup (1.6x-2.1x) at batch size one. Direction is robust across configurations and both tasks; magnitude is not, and depends heavily on batch size and task. The native executable itself was separately timed (Section 6): an initial 6.7-7.2 s per cold invocation was traced mostly to an unaccelerated self-written SHA-256 fingerprinting implementation, not primarily to Rosetta 2 translation; fixing it and building a working arm64-native binary (the executor's only x86-64-assembly dependency has no ISA-specific instructions) brought cold-invocation latency to roughly 4.3-5.0 s (x86_64-under-Rosetta) and 1.3-1.6 s (arm64-native), the latter about 2x the PyTorch reference numbers above rather than an order of magnitude. Three independent attempts to remove a further suspected redundancy (the bundle is read once to fingerprint and again to parse) showed no measured improvement and are not included. Decomposing the native transfer itself into fixed (sender prefill + import) and marginal (one receiver query) cost, excluding weight loading, finds it faster than the PyTorch-reference-environment equivalent on both architectures (364/14 ms arm64, 441/17 ms x86_64-under-Rosetta, vs. 590/39 ms reference) — the earlier native-slower finding was a property of cold-process weight loading, not of the transfer computation. No native re-prefill counterpart exists to decompose the same way, so no native breakeven-query-count or cache-transfer-vs-reprefill comparison is possible yet. Peak-memory, communication savings, and precision/compression tradeoffs are not measured. |

Single-fact transfer and earlier scalar/per-channel/dense hidden-state experiments belong to the wider exploratory history, not the quantitative evidence set of this manuscript. We do not infer their final status from the supplied narrative inventory or silently fold them into a KV-adapter hierarchy. The source ledger intentionally identifies the experiments supporting this report rather than presenting the entire repository as a single controlled study.
